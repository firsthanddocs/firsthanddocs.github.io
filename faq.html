Questions

Straight answers, before you call.

Most notary trouble comes from rules nobody explains until you're standing there with a document that can't be signed. Here's what actually governs a California notarization, in plain language.

Identification

What ID do I need?

California requires a current, government-issued photo ID that includes your signature and a physical description. The ones I see most often:

  • California driver license or state ID card
  • US passport or passport card
  • Driver license or ID from another US state
  • US military ID
  • Foreign passport, if stamped by US Citizenship and Immigration Services
  • Consular ID from an accredited consulate
  • Inmate ID issued by CDCR, for a signer in custody

The ID must be current, or have been issued within the past five years. A student ID, employee badge, birth certificate, or Social Security card will not work, no matter how official they look.

My license is expired. Am I out of luck?

Not necessarily, but read this carefully. An expired ID is acceptable only if it was issued within the last five years. A license that expired last month but was issued seven years ago does not qualify. A license issued three years ago that expired last month does.

If your ID doesn't work, the fallback is credible identifying witnesses: one person who personally knows you and also knows me, or two people who personally know you and have their own acceptable ID. They swear under oath to your identity. It's a real option and it works, but it takes arranging in advance.

Tell me about the ID situation when you call. Solving it on the phone is free. Discovering it at your kitchen table costs us both a trip.

The name on my ID doesn't match my document. Now what?

The rule of thumb: the name on the document can be shorter than, or equal to, the name on your ID. It cannot be longer.

If your ID says "Robert James Miller," you can sign as "Robert Miller" or "Robert J. Miller." If your ID says "Robert Miller" and the document says "Robert James Miller," that's a problem, because I can't verify the middle name from your ID.

Maiden names, married names, and hyphenations trip this up constantly. If there's a mismatch, call before the appointment so we can sort out whether it works.

The Appointment

Does the signer have to be physically present?

Yes. Every signer whose signature is being notarized must appear in person, in front of me, at the time of the notarization. There is no exception for a spouse, an adult child, a business partner, or someone holding a power of attorney over the signer.

You cannot bring me a document your father already signed at home. He and I have to be in the same room.

Should I sign the document before you arrive?

For an acknowledgment, you may sign beforehand, as long as you personally acknowledge the signature to me in person.

For a jurat, no. You must sign in my presence and take an oath or affirmation.

Since most people don't know which one their document needs, the safe answer is: don't sign until I'm there. If it turns out to be an acknowledgment, nothing is lost.

Can you notarize for someone in a hospital or care facility?

Yes, and it's a meaningful part of what I do. Hospitals, hospice, skilled nursing, memory care, and rehab facilities are all normal calls.

What the signer needs to be able to do:

  • Be awake and aware of what they're signing
  • Communicate willingness on their own, without someone answering for them
  • Sign, or direct a signature by mark with two witnesses present
  • Produce acceptable ID, or arrange credible witnesses

These appointments are often family situations under real time pressure. I've spent a career in rooms like that. Call and tell me what's happening, and I'll tell you honestly whether it can be done.

What if the signer can't hold a pen?

California allows a signature by mark. The signer makes an X or any mark they can manage, two witnesses observe, one witness writes the signer's name beside the mark, and both witnesses sign. The signer must still be present, aware, and willing.

Tell me in advance so I can arrange the witnesses and complete the certificate correctly.

What if the signer doesn't speak English?

I have to communicate directly with the signer, in a language we both understand, to confirm they know what they're signing and are doing it willingly. California does not permit a notary to use a translator or interpreter for that conversation.

The document itself may be in any language. The conversation between us cannot go through a third party.

When a Notary Has to Say No

Why would you refuse to notarize my document?

This is the question behind most late-night searching, so here's the honest list of what actually stops a notarization:

  • ID doesn't meet the standard. Not current, or issued more than five years ago, or missing a photo, signature, or physical description.
  • The signer isn't present. No exceptions.
  • Blanks in the document. Empty spaces have to be filled in or struck through before I can proceed.
  • The signer doesn't understand what they're signing. If someone is confused, heavily medicated, or can't describe the document in their own words, I have to stop.
  • The signer seems pressured. If someone in the room is answering for them or pushing them, that's a stop.
  • No certificate wording. If the document has no notarial certificate and nobody can tell you which act it needs, I'm not allowed to choose for you.
  • I have a beneficial interest. I can't notarize anything I'm a party to or stand to gain from.
  • Certain vital records. I can't certify copies of birth, death, or marriage certificates. Those come from the county recorder or the state.

A refusal is almost never about you. It's the law leaving me nowhere to stand. And a notary who bends on any of these is a notary whose signature won't hold up when it matters.

The last notary turned me away. Can you do it?

Maybe, and it's worth a phone call to find out. Sometimes the previous refusal was correct and no notary in California can help until something changes, like getting a current ID. Sometimes it was a fixable problem, like a missing certificate the receiving agency can supply, or a bank notary who wasn't permitted to handle that document type.

Tell me what happened and what they told you. If it can't be fixed, I'll say so on the phone instead of driving out and charging you for the trip.

Can you tell me which notarial act I need?

No, and I want to explain why, because it frustrates people.

California law prohibits a notary who isn't an attorney from advising which act to use, or from helping draft or complete your document. Doing so is the unauthorized practice of law. It isn't caution on my part. It's a hard line.

Most documents arrive with the certificate wording already on them, so this rarely comes up. When it doesn't, whoever is going to receive the document — the court, the bank, the county, the agency, your attorney — has to tell you what they require. Once you know, I can complete it.

Fees & Logistics

What does it cost?

Two separate numbers, added together. California requires that they stay separate on the receipt.

$15 per signature notarized. The state sets this maximum. Two signers on one document is two signatures. One signer on three documents is three signatures.

Plus a travel fee, based on where you are:

  • Zone 1 — $20. Morro Bay, Los Osos, Baywood, Cayucos
  • Zone 2 — $30. San Luis Obispo, Atascadero, Cambria, San Simeon
  • Zone 3 — $40. Paso Robles, San Miguel, Arroyo Grande, Grover Beach, Pismo Beach, Avila Beach, Santa Margarita, Creston
  • Zone 4 — $50. Nipomo, Guadalupe, Santa Maria, Orcutt, Shandon, Cholame
  • Zone 5 — $60. Lompoc, Los Alamos, Vandenberg

So one signature at your kitchen table in Los Osos is $15 + $20 = $35 total.

Hospital, hospice, and care facility visits are quoted individually. After-hours, holiday, and same-day urgent calls may carry an additional fee. Either way, you'll have the full number before I book the appointment.

How do I pay?

Card or tap-to-pay at the appointment, or cash. Firms with recurring volume can be invoiced.

What are your hours, and how fast can you come?

Monday through Saturday, 9am to 6pm. Often same day, frequently within a few hours around Morro Bay, Los Osos, San Luis Obispo, and Cayucos.

Outside those hours:

  • Sundays — add $25
  • After 6pm — add $25
  • Christmas, Thanksgiving, Easter — add $75

Everything else is a normal business day. New Year's, July 4th, Labor Day, Memorial Day, and the rest of the calendar — call me, it's a regular appointment at regular rates.

Hospital, hospice, and care facility visits are quoted individually, and they move to the front of the line whenever I can manage it. If someone needs a directive signed at a bedside on a holiday, call me and we'll work it out.

Do you keep a copy of my document?

No. Your documents stay with you. California requires me to keep a journal recording the date, the type of act, the document type, the signer's name, how identity was established, and the signer's thumbprint for certain real property documents. That journal is my legal record. It isn't a copy of your paperwork.
